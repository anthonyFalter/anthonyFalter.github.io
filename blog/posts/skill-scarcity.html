Which tech skills take the longest to fill?

August 9, 2026, 1:56 PM · Ross Elopre

In the current technology landscape, change often happens within months. Which makes certain skillset obsolete in terms of functionality due to the presence of a new, more efficient and cost-effective tools. Despite this rapid evolution in terms of technology used, companies still prefer to stay in their current technology ecosystem because the cost of migrating to a new framework outweighs the profit and products they are able to produce within the time window. Which, is a nice way to tell the conflict of: Innovation Vs. Cost of Migration



What exactly are these skills?

(This visualization is interactive, try hovering or clicking on each one)

We can see at the viz (short for visualization) that we have MuleSoft at the very top of rankings.

What exactly is MuleSoft?

MuleSoft is an integration platform used by enterprises to connect disparate software, applications, and legacy systems via Application Programmer Interface or API which is like a digital messenger that requests from a system, delivers it to another and brings the response back.



Why does it top the rank as the hardest tech skill to fill?

It is basically an expensive enterprise product mainly used by large corporations. Engineers don't typically learn it on their own for fun; they learn it on the job. This creates a small, highly specialized talent pool.

While MuleSoft takes the crown, we can actually see a pattern as to why they are in the list:

  • Enterprise & Niche Infrastructure (e.g., Supabase, Cassandra, Spark): These backend and big-data tools require complex architecture knowledge that candidates usually only gain by working and rarely at schools.
  • Bleeding-Edge Tech (e.g., AI Agents, XGBoost): Machine learning and autonomous AI are moving faster than traditional educational curriculums, in which the top candidates are usually masters of their craft, or people who are really passionate in these fields.
  • High-Barrier Systems Programming (e.g., C++, Kotlin, Ruby): Whether it's managing low-level memory in C++ or maintaining massive legacy codebases in Ruby, these languages require years of deep technical mastery.

In short, the hardest skills to hire aren't just "hard to learn", they are skills where experience is hard to grasp because of its complexity, access to its ecosystem, or its requirement of being able to keep up due to sheer rapid growth.



How far are companies willing to go just to secure a talent that has these skills?



Difficulty in hiring is one thing, but how does scarcity in these fields translate into compensation? From the data above, we can see that there is a massive gap between enterprise-related and backend frameworks.

  • The Integration & Platform Tax (MuleSoft @ +87.2%): Because MuleSoft sits at the heart of enterprise digital transformations, companies are willing to pay almost double the standard market rate to secure experienced developers.
  • High-Scarcity Core Systems (Kotlin @ +70.7%, Cassandra @ +67.6%): Mobile infrastructure and distributed big-data systems command massive premiums because system downtime or poor architecture costs companies far more than high tech salaries.
  • Emerging Tech Premium (AI Agents @ +58.9%): Emerging fields like AI agents command a ~60% premium as companies rush to build modern, automated workflows before their competitors do.

And then, it drops starting from ~14.45% of XGBoost onwards.



Why is that?

The data proves that high demand alone doesn't guarantee a top-tier paycheck unless the barrier to entry is equally steep. It also follows the law of Supply and Demand perfectly!
